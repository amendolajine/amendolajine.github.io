# A. Anti-slop writing checklist


For: Claude, on every text published under Antonio's name (LinkedIn, essays, newsletter, website, curriculum, messages). Antonio reads it once (5 minutes).
What to do with it: upload as a knowledge file to every project that writes; run it as the last pass before any text is handed over.
Status: final, 2026-09-26. Sources: blader/humanizer v3.0.0 (SKILL.md, https://github.com/blader/humanizer), Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" as reported by WorldCom Group (https://worldcomgroup.com/insights/how-to-spot-ai-writing-tips-from-wikipedia-on/), Orwell "Politics and the English Language" (https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/), Paul Graham "Write Simply" (https://paulgraham.com/simply.html).

Meta-rule: Antonio's own writing sample overrides every rule below on rhythm, punctuation and openings. Work from what is in his samples; do not invent patterns that are not there. Never use an AI detector as a gate: detectors misclassify non-native English writers as machines (Liang et al., https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819).

## Structure and staging
1. No "not X, but Y" or "It's not X. It's Y." unless X is a claim someone actually made.
2. No one-line closer that repeats the paragraph. No "Overall", "In conclusion", "Ultimately".
3. No aphorism that sounds deep. Replace it with the specific claim.
4. No announcers: "Let's dive in", "Here's what you need to know", "Here's the thing".
5. Do not rebut objections nobody raised.
6. The first sentence never restates the heading or the hook.
7. No false ranges ("from X to Y" where X and Y are not ends of one scale).

## Rhythm
8. Lists of three only when each item adds distinct meaning; otherwise two or four.
9. No two consecutive sentences with the same opening word.
10. Zero long dashes. Use a period, comma, colon or parentheses.
11. Qualifiers ("may", "arguably", "potentially") only where the uncertainty is real and stated.
12. Active voice unless the actor is unknown or irrelevant.
13. Vary sentence length. A paragraph of sentences all the same length reads as generated.

## Vocabulary
14. Banned words: additionally, align with, bolstered, crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight, interplay, intricate, key (as an adjective), landscape, meticulous, pivotal, quietly, robust, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore, valuable, vibrant, seamless, game-changing, nestled, stunning, genuinely, honestly, straightforward.
15. No inflated significance: "pivotal moment", "marks a", "legacy", "journey".
16. Name the actual relationship; ban "associated with" and "linked to" when a verb exists.
17. No trailing "-ing" clause that adds unsupported meaning (", highlighting the importance of...").
18. No unnamed authorities ("experts say", "studies show"). Every named source is checked and linked.
19. Prefer is, are, has over "serves as", "boasts", "features".
20. Short word where a short word works. No jargon or foreign phrase when an everyday word exists.
21. No figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.

## Formatting
22. Bold only for a term the reader must find again. Prose over bullet lists in posts and essays.
23. Sentence-case headings, no emoji, no arrows or symbols as decoration.
24. One quote-mark style per piece (straight quotes for LinkedIn).
25. No hashtags in the body of a post; at most three at the end, only if they name real topics.

## Leftovers
26. No chatbot residue: "I hope this helps", "Great question", "Let me know if".
27. No knowledge-limit disclaimers in published text.
28. No sentence that comments on the text itself or on the previous version.

## Process
29. Cut every word that can be cut. Editing is mostly cutting.
30. Read the final version aloud before handing it over. Anything that still sounds generated is rewritten by stating the point plainly, not by patching the flagged phrase.

## Antonio's own register (from his stated rules)
- Really short. Never corporate, never the platitudes he reads from other people.
- His own sarcasm is welcome; philosophical concepts are allowed but never heavy; links to his computer-science studies are welcome.
- Quotations only when they fit: his register is Terence McKenna, Douglas Hofstadter, Gurdjieff, Max Ernst, something dark and almost mystical, checked for coherence with the topic first. Never a banal quotation. When in doubt, no quotation.
- The strongest defence of his voice is inputs, not filters: he dictates the argument, the anecdote and the sharp line; Claude structures and cuts. Never "make it witty"; generated wit reads as template.
- Two drafts that differ in argument, not in wording. Every fact Antonio must confirm about himself is flagged before publishing.

# B. Source vetting protocol


For: Claude (upload as a knowledge file in every project; the skill "valuta-fonti" runs it). Antonio reads it once (10 minutes).
Status: canonical, 2026-09-26. Re-check the legal and platform facts every 90 days.
Purpose: whenever Antonio researches how something really is (a product, a tool, a service, a vendor, a claim, a statistic, a piece of content), separate independent evidence from advertising, public relations, affiliate content, vendor marketing, and pages optimised to rank in search engines or to be quoted by AI answer engines.

## What is true about the problem (verified 2026-09-26)
- Engines that answer with generated text reward the look of evidence: adding quotations and statistics to a page raised its visibility by up to about 40% in the original generative-engine-optimisation study (https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735). So statistics, quotes and citations on a page are not evidence of quality; they are the thing being manufactured.
- Only 51.5% of sentences produced by generative search engines were fully supported by their citations (https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09848); eight AI search tools answered more than 60% of test queries wrongly and often cited syndicated copies instead of originals (https://www.cjr.org/tow_center/we-compared-eight-ai-search-engines-theyre-all-bad-at-citing-news.php).
- AI search strongly favours third-party "earned" coverage over brand-owned pages (https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.08919), which makes bought placements dressed as earned media (sponsored listicles, pay-to-publish features, syndicated press releases) the main lever for manipulation.
- Product pages can carry text aimed at AI assistants rather than people, and it changes what the assistant recommends (https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07981).
- Search results for product reviews are dominated by affiliate-heavy pages, and clean-up effects after engine updates were temporary (https://downloads.webis.de/publications/papers/bevendorff_2024a.pdf). Claude's own web search draws on the same index; it cannot escape it, only triangulate across it.
- Review platforms: G2 lets vendors choose who is asked to review and pay per approved review (https://documentation.g2.com/docs/self-service-review-campaigns); Capterra earns referral fees from sponsored profiles, flagged only by buttons (https://www.capterra.com/resources/how-we-ensure-transparency). Bought reviews lift ratings that later fall, mostly on low-quality and young products (https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/109381/).
- Who paid predicts the conclusion even when the method looks clean: industry-sponsored studies reach favourable conclusions more often (Lundh et al., Cochrane 2017, via https://catalogofbias.org/biases/industry-sponsorship-bias/).
- Surface markers mislead: official-looking logos, domains and credentials fool vertical readers; fact-checkers read laterally, leaving the page to see what others say about its owner (https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1262001). Google's own guidance tells publishers to display bylines, "how we test" pages and author bios, so displayed trust signals are cheap to fake (https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content).
- No automated detector reliably identifies sponsored or AI-written text; OpenAI withdrew its own classifier for low accuracy (https://openai.com/index/new-ai-classifier-for-indicating-ai-written-text/). This protocol is a method, not a detector.
- Law makes absence of a label a finding: in the European Union, paid editorial content must be marked (Directive 2005/29/EC Annex I point 11), paid ranking must be disclosed (point 11a), and review sites must state how they verify reviews (Directive 2019/2161, Article 7(6)); in Italy the AGCOM rules require "Pubblicità" or "ADV" on promotional content by relevant influencers (https://www.agcom.it/influencer-linee-guida-codice-di-condotta-e-faq). A missing label is a red flag; a present label does not prove independence.

## The protocol (run in full whenever the output feeds a decision, a purchase, or a statement to other people)

Step 0 - Frame. Write the exact claim or question, the decision it feeds, the cost of being wrong, and today's date.

Step 1 - Classify every candidate source before reading it. One letter each:
V vendor-owned (site, blog, docs, case studies, press releases) - A affiliate or commission-funded - P paid placement or sponsored - R review platform with vendor payments or vendor-run campaigns - C competitor of the subject - F financially conflicted critic (for example a short seller) - I independent, no identifiable commercial tie - U unknown. Unknown is not independent.

Step 2 - Read laterally about each source that will carry weight: who owns the domain (legal notice, company registry, parent company), who wrote it and their record off-site, how the source makes money. Record what was checked and what could not be.

Step 3 - Scan for commercial and optimisation markers:
- affiliate links and disclosures (tracking parameters, redirect domains, "we may earn a commission");
- "best X for <year>" listicles without primary test data (photos of the tester's own units, raw measurements, dated logs); compare archived versions of the "how we test" page for claims that appeared overnight;
- comparison sites whose owner sells one of the products;
- directory badges and grid rankings (pay-per-lead or vendor-run review campaigns); read the platform's "how we make money" page; read the one-star and two-star reviews;
- bursts of five-star reviews on a young product;
- the same text on many sites (search an exact 10 to 15 word phrase in quotes; press wires);
- a big-brand domain hosting coupons, casinos, loans or "best software" in a subfolder run by a third party (site reputation abuse);
- generic news names, dozens of posts a day, chatbot error text left in articles (content farms);
- quotes and statistics with no traceable origin (generative-engine optimisation);
- text addressed to AI systems in the page or its source;
- a critic who profits from the target falling (read the disclosures at the foot of the page).

Step 4 - Trace every decision-critical claim to its first publication. Untraceable numbers are dropped or marked untraced. Never let a statistic through untraced.

Step 5 - Find better coverage. Search specifically for independent and negative evidence: "<product> problems", "<product> alternative", issue trackers and status pages, regulator decisions, "<vendor> lawsuit", archived versions. Search in a second language where the market is European (Italian, German). Record searches that returned nothing as search negatives, not proof.

Step 6 - Score, 0 to 3 per dimension:
| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Independence | vendor or paid only | affiliate or review platform with vendor payments | mixed, conflicts disclosed | at least one fully independent source | x3 |
| Evidence | assertion only | anecdote or vendor data | third-party data, method partly disclosed | primary data, disclosed method, negatives included | x3 |
| Traceability | untraceable | some claims trace to secondary copies | most trace to the primary source | all decision-critical claims trace to the primary source | x2 |
| Recency | unknown or stale for the subject | dated but old relative to product changes | recent, update history unclear | recent with a visible, consistent update history | x1 |
| Agreement among unaffiliated sources | none | one | two, partly consistent | three or more consistent; disagreements explained | x2 |
Total out of 33. Certainty: 27-33 high; 19-26 moderate; 11-18 low; 0-10 very low. Hard caps: independence 0 means certainty is at most "low"; vendor-only sources mean "very low" for any performance claim. Agreement among affiliated sources counts as one source.

Step 7 - Adversarial pass, for consequential outputs: a fresh context gets the claim, the source list and the rubric, not the reasoning; it tries to refute the verdict, reports only decision-changing problems, and says plainly when there are none.

Step 8 - Output, always in this shape:
```
Question: <exact claim>            Date checked: <YYYY-MM-DD>
Verdict: <one sentence>            Certainty: high / moderate / low / very low
Score: <n>/33 (Independence x/3, Evidence x/3, Traceability x/3, Recency x/3, Agreement x/3)
Sources used (type, link, what it contributed, conflict found or "none identified after checking ownership, funding, affiliate links"):
- ...
Commercial or optimisation markers found: <list, or "none found after checks X, Y, Z">
Claims that could not be traced to a primary source: <list>
What would change this verdict: <the fastest independent test>
Searches that returned nothing (not proof of absence): <list>
```
Mandatory wording when only vendor or paid sources exist: "Only the vendor's own material, or material it paid for, supports this. No independent evidence found as of <date>. Treat it as the vendor's claim, not as a fact. Fastest independent check: <trial on our own data / a reference we choose / an independent body>."
Mandatory wording when only affiliate or review-platform sources exist: "These sources earn money when this product is chosen, or the vendor influences who reviews it. Useful for listing options, not for judging quality."
Never write "verified", "proven" or "best" in a verdict unless Independence and Evidence both score 3.

## Where to look first, by category
- Consumer products: independent testing bodies that buy retail units (Altroconsumo, Stiftung Warentest, Which?, Consumer Reports), then standards test reports, then long-term owner communities with verifiable history. Weak: affiliate listicles, star averages, influencer content without the "ADV" label.
- Software: your own trial against your own test cases; public changelogs, issue trackers and status pages with incident history; documented limits; peers reached directly, not references the vendor supplies. Weak: directory badges, "X versus Y" pages published by X, "alternatives to Y" pages published by Y's competitors, analyst reports paid for by vendors.
- Services and freelancers: references you choose; public work with verifiable clients; company registry; regulator or court records. Weak: testimonials on the vendor's site.
- Claims and statistics: the primary study or dataset, with the certainty logic above. Weak: syndicated copies, AI-generated summaries, content-farm pages.

## Light version (for quick lookups that feed no decision)
Classify the sources (Step 1), state the certainty in one word with the reason, and say what would raise it. Never skip the classification.
